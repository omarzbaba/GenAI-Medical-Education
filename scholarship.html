Pillar 3 of 4

Scholarship

Clinician-educators are also scholars — designing studies, scoping literature, drafting manuscripts, writing grants, often in fragments of protected time. This is where AI use collides most directly with formal rules: authorship criteria, disclosure requirements, and confidentiality norms written specifically for LLMs. The organizing principle is narrower than in the other pillars: AI can accelerate the scaffolding of scholarly work, but the intellectual claims, the data, and the accountability remain human.

ICMJE and COPE are explicit: AI cannot be an author, because it cannot take responsibility for the work. Human authors remain accountable for AI-assisted content, and disclosure is increasingly mandatory.

Interactive walkthrough

The six-step grant-development sequence

The hard part of a grant is often not producing text but articulating the significance of work you know intimately — deep immersion makes it easy to assume reviewers share your background. Rather than asking a model to draft a Specific Aims page immediately, the manuscript's sequence (Table 5, Figure 3) uses AI to improve the scientific communication before any drafting begins. Run it in order, in a single conversation.

Worked sequence

From immersed investigator to legible proposal

Step 1 · Build context

Surface the core scientific narrative

I am developing a grant proposal. Based only on the information I
provide, summarize the scientific problem, knowledge gap, novelty,
and potential impact. Clearly distinguish established findings from
hypotheses.

Supply non-confidential context only. "Based only on the information I provide" keeps the model from decorating your science with invented background.

Step 2 · Test understanding

The model explains the project back

Explain this project back to me as if you were describing it to an
NIH study-section reviewer outside my specialty. What do you think
the project is really about, and what is its central innovation?
The diagnostic signal

If the model misstates the central hypothesis, innovation, or significance despite detailed background, a reviewer unfamiliar with the field is likely to stumble in the same place — a signal that the framing needs work, not the prose.

Step 3 · Expand significance

Widen the scientific perspective

Suggest related diseases, biomarkers, biological pathways,
technologies, or clinical applications that could strengthen the
significance of this proposal. Clearly identify which suggestions
require literature verification.

An investigator studying a prostate-cancer biomarker may find that related markers or analytic approaches were explored in breast, lung, or colorectal disease.

Verification rule

These connections are hypotheses to pursue in the primary literature, not established facts — each requires verification before it enters an application.

Step 4 · Reviewer critique

Stress-test before a real reviewer does

Act as an experienced grant reviewer. Identify the three biggest
weaknesses in my significance argument. What evidence is missing?
Which claims appear overstated?

Confirm each weakness against your preliminary data before restructuring. And mind rubric currency: review frameworks are revised on their own schedule — NIH has proposed replacing overall impact scores and percentiles with categories — so name the version you are simulating.

Step 5 · Build the story

Impose the canonical arc

Organize my proposal into the following structure: Important
Problem → Knowledge Gap → Preliminary Data → Central Hypothesis →
Specific Aims → Expected Impact. Explain the purpose of each
section and ensure each leads logically to the next.

Read the result for the weakest hand-off between sections — that is where a reviewer will stop believing. Strengthen the science there before drafting.

Step 6 · Draft the framework

A Specific Aims scaffold, flagged for human input

Draft a Specific Aims framework using the structure above. Mark
every place where additional preliminary data, citations, or
investigator interpretation is required. Do not fabricate
references or scientific findings.
The closing principle

The applicant rewrites the scaffold with real preliminary data and domain judgment. The model reduces blank-page time; it does not supply the science. Every scientific claim, citation, and interpretation is independently verified before it enters the proposal — and confidential or unpublished material never enters a public consumer tool.

The editorial line

Pre-submission critique — three binding conditions

Asking a model to critique your unsubmitted draft is a rehearsal of peer review, not a substitute for it. The manuscript bounds it three ways:

  1. Directional. Your own unsubmitted work only. A reviewer must never run it on a manuscript assigned to them — confidential by editorial policy.
  2. Deployment. An unsubmitted manuscript is unpublished intellectual property — use an institutional or sanctioned tool whose terms exclude training, not a public consumer account.
  3. Interpretive. The model anticipates objections a competent reviewer would raise — not the misreadings a reviewer should not raise. Concordance with human review is a ceiling, not a gold standard; silence is not clearance.

The instruction to diagnose rather than rewrite is deliberate: it keeps your voice, keeps revision a learning event, and keeps disclosure simple, because no generated prose enters the manuscript. And simulated critique does not replace mentorship — an experienced colleague reads for what a model cannot.

Disclosure, in practice

A defensible disclosure identifies the tool and its role in the manner the target journal requires. The manuscript's own working example:

During the preparation of this work the authors used ChatGPT (OpenAI) to improve the readability and language of the Introduction and Discussion. After using this tool, the authors reviewed and edited the content as needed and take full responsibility for the content of the publication.
