Pillar 1 of 4

Learning

The trainee is at the keyboard. This pillar covers the self-directed study that carries a clinician from board preparation through a career of tracking new markers and successive classifications — with AI as a high-availability tutor, not a source of truth.

What the evidence supports

LLMs handle on-demand concept clarification well: re-deriving diagnostic logic, contrasting two entities, restating a pattern in plain language. Frontier models now outperform humans on many written board-style items. But performance is uneven across modalities — the same tools that score well on written stems falter on image-anchored content, and generated practice questions can lack a defensible correct answer. The practical stance: a first-pass tutor within bounds set by tier, modality, and difficulty.

A first-year resident meets a transfusion reaction

This walkthrough reproduces the manuscript's worked example (Supplementary Table S3) — a single, real prompt run with the verification step applied. Model outputs are non-deterministic and version-dependent: reproduce and re-verify this in your own tool.

Scenario

Concept explanation, calibrated to level — then verified

Step 1 · The situation

Night float, first month of clinical pathology

A first-year CP resident gets a call about a possible transfusion reaction. They know the term "acute hemolytic transfusion reaction" from medical school; they cannot yet reconstruct the workup from first principles. Tomorrow they want the concept solid. A textbook chapter is the right first encounter with a totally unfamiliar topic — but this is a re-explanation need, which is exactly where an LLM earns its place as a tutor.

Attempt first. Before prompting, the resident writes down their own version of the workup. The framework's second design principle — attempt-first prompting — turns the model's answer into a critique of their reasoning rather than a substitute for it.

Step 2 · The prompt

Role, context, task, format — in one calibrated ask

Note what the prompt pins down: the audience level ("first-year CP resident"), the scope (pathophysiology, initial workup, the two results that most change management), and the format ("big picture, then define each key term"). Structure is not decoration — it is what keeps the output at your level instead of the model's default. 

Explain the diagnostic approach to a suspected acute hemolytic
transfusion reaction for a first-year CP resident: pathophysiology,
the initial workup, and the two results that most change management.
Keep the big picture, then define each key term.

Step 3 · The output (real recorded run)

Fluent, organized — and not yet trusted

From the manuscript's records: Claude Opus 4.8 (Anthropic), run August 5, 2026. Excerpt, exactly as recorded:

Model output — excerpt

The big picture. An acute hemolytic transfusion reaction is the rapid destruction of transfused red cells, classically from ABO-incompatible blood — recipient antibodies bind donor cells, activate complement, and lyse them intravascularly. Severe cases are usually a patient-identification error, so the investigation is as much a safety audit as a laboratory workup.

[Pathophysiology and initial-workup paragraphs omitted for space.]

The two results that most change management. (1) Confirmation of an ABO discrepancy via clerical recheck and repeat typing — establishes the diagnosis and flags that a second patient may be at risk. (2) Direct evidence of intravascular hemolysis (hemoglobinemia on plasma inspection, positive DAT) — confirms the reaction is hemolytic and commits to fluids, renal monitoring, and DIC surveillance.

Step 4 · The verification step

The step that makes the tool safe

The prompt's library entry names exactly what to check — because models often blur acute hemolytic with febrile non-hemolytic reactions and misstate which test is confirmatory.

Verification, as applied in the manuscript

Output preserved the acute-hemolytic vs. febrile-non-hemolytic distinction that models often blur; workup steps and the ABO-incompatibility mechanism confirmed against a transfusion-medicine reference.

Only after this check does the explanation enter the resident's notes. The person prompting the model is the person who verifies its output.

Step 5 · The judgment call

Where clinical judgment enters

Recorded judgment call

The prompt asks for the two results that most change management; the choice is not unique, and the verification step is where clinical judgment is applied.

This is the quiet lesson of the whole pillar: a well-formed prompt produces a defensible answer, not the answer. An expert might have chosen differently — and knowing that requires exactly the foundational knowledge the tool cannot supply on demand.

Guardrails for this pillar

Three habits, from the manuscript, that matter more than allegiance to any product:

  • Verify factual claims against primary sources. Performance declines on harder content; capable systems fabricate references and reproduce outdated guidance.
  • Calibrate trust to the task. Text-based knowledge questions are answered far more reliably than image-anchored ones.
  • Prefer domain-specific multimodal tools for image-anchored questions. A text-only LLM is a poor arbiter of a photomicrograph. See the four tool tiers →

And the hazard specific to learners: overreliance. Foundational competence is the precondition for using AI safely — not one more thing AI can supply. The three skill-formation principles →
