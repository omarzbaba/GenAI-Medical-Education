Digital companion to the manuscript Generative AI in Medical Education and Scholarship: A Practical Framework and Prompt Toolkit from Pathology

Trainees and educators already use ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, and Gemini for studying, teaching, literature scoping, and manuscript writing — mostly improvised, with little formal training. This companion turns the manuscript's framework into something you can walk through: four pillars, guided scenario demos, and a library of 102 prompts, each paired with its intent, expected output, failure modes, and an explicit human-verification step.

Across all four pillars, AI shifts effort without shifting accountability.

By Omar Z. Baba, MD, Tanmay P. Lele, PhD, & Srikar Chamala, PhD. Worked through in pathology — a demanding case, image-anchored and fast-moving — but written for medical education broadly.

The working loop: task → tool → prompt → verify

The durable skill this framework teaches is not familiarity with any product — it is appraisal. Every workflow in this companion runs the same loop:

1 · Match the tool to the task

Tier selection is a design decision, not a default.

A general-purpose LLM is a reasonable first-pass tutor for a text concept and a poor arbiter of an image. Four tool tiers, with best-fit tasks, are laid out in the guardrails section.

2 · Prompt with structure

Role · context · task · format · verification cue.

Prompt structure materially affects output quality. Try the interactive prompt anatomy — toggle a component off and watch what the model has to assume.

3 · Verify before you use

Every factual assertion is provisional until checked.

Each of the 102 library prompts names specifically what to verify before trusting the output. The person prompting the model is the person who verifies its output.

A caveat that applies to every prompt on this site. Generative models are non-deterministic and version-dependent: the same prompt yields different responses across tools, accounts, and dates. The prompts here are illustrative scaffolds to adapt and re-verify in your own tool — not fixed recipes. The verification habit, rather than any specific wording, is the transferable skill.

For trainees: the overreliance paradox

The verification that makes these tools safe presupposes the knowledge to judge what is being verified. A practicing clinician brings that knowledge; a trainee is still assembling it.

Used too early, an answer-supplying tool can prevent the very competence it appears to support.

The manuscript answers with three design principles — foundation-first, attempt-first prompting, and staged autonomy. Explore the skill-formation principles →

Deskilling

Erosion of a competence an experienced clinician already holds, through over-reliance or disuse.

Mis-skilling

Uncritical acceptance of AI errors, so the trainee internalises flawed knowledge as fact.

Never-skilling

A foundational competence that never forms at all, because AI performed the task during the formative window.
