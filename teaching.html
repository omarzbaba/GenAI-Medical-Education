Pillar 2 of 4 — the anchor

Teaching, Curriculum & Assessment

Now the educator is at the keyboard. Faculty carry a recurring drafting burden — rotation blueprints, session objectives, teaching cases, item banks — most of it rebuilt from scratch each year. Generative AI shifts the starting point from a blank page to a reviewable draft. The evidence divides cleanly along one line: LLMs are usable content drafters and unreliable content authorities.

The workflow that follows holds across every task in this pillar: the model proposes, and a content expert disposes.

Interactive walkthrough

Draft → Review → Extend: building a teaching case

Case writing is where the drafter role pays off most visibly, because a good vignette is mostly structure a model can assemble and a faculty member can correct. Follow one case from prompt to classroom — including the error the review step exists to catch.

Scenario

A transfusion-medicine session, PGY-2 level

Step 1 · Draft — the prompt

The model generates a skeleton

The prompt (from the manuscript) constrains structure, level, and internal consistency — and labels the case as hypothetical from the start: 

Create a PGY-2-level teaching case of a suspected acute hemolytic
transfusion reaction. Include a brief clinical history, an event
timeline, the key pre- and post-transfusion laboratory findings
(DAT, haptoglobin, LDH, plasma-free hemoglobin, urinalysis), and
three teaching points. Keep all values internally consistent and
label this as a hypothetical, de-identified case.

Step 2 · The draft comes back

Illustrative output — with a defect planted where models really fail

An abridged draft of the kind a model returns. Read the laboratory table carefully — one value is wrong in a way that fluent prose makes easy to miss. (Illustrative example, not a recorded run; reproduce in your own tool.)

Model output — abridged

Hypothetical, de-identified teaching case. A 62-year-old undergoing hip revision receives a unit of packed red cells. Fifteen minutes in: rigors, flank pain, temperature 38.9°C, dark urine. Transfusion stopped; clerical check initiated…

Step 3 · Review — the expert gate

Review is not optional polishing

The educator confirms the results, the timeline, and the clerical-check narrative are mutually consistent, adjusts values to local reference ranges, and inserts institutional workup policy — the details a text-trained model has no reliable access to. Here, the check catches the planted defect:

Caught at review

Haptoglobin rising (110 → 168 mg/dL) during acute intravascular hemolysis is backwards. Haptoglobin binds free hemoglobin and is consumed — it should fall, typically to undetectable. Every other value moves the right way, which is exactly what makes the error dangerous: a learner drilled on this case would absorb an inverted lab pattern as fact (mis-skilling).

Corrected before use

Haptoglobin 1 h post-transfusion corrected to <8 mg/dL (undetectable); values re-checked against local reference ranges; institutional transfusion-reaction workup policy inserted into the timeline.

Step 4 · Extend

Only now are the discussion questions layered on

Extend adds the questions that carry the learning — asked of the corrected case:

Extension questions

“What clerical checks should have caught this before issue?”
“What further testing confirms the diagnosis?”
“How would the picture differ in a febrile non-hemolytic reaction?”

Structured generation paired with expert curation — never treated as finished output — is how generated cases are used safely. The scalability is real; the oversight is what makes it safe. One more screen: check demographic details for unnecessary or stereotyped attributes before the case reaches learners.

Second demo

The MCQ expert gate

Item generation is the most-studied task in this pillar, and the literature reads honestly in both directions: models draft items dramatically faster than human authors, and generated items frequently lack a single defensible correct answer or carry confidently misleading rationales. A distractor that is subtly also correct does more harm than a missing item — it teaches the wrong thing under the authority of an answer key.

Scenario

Five pre-analytical-error MCQs, under review

Step 1 · The constrained prompt

Even the prompt anticipates the failure mode

Write 5 single-best-answer MCQs on pre-analytical error in the
clinical laboratory at the application level of Bloom's taxonomy.
For each item give the answer key and a one-sentence rationale for
why each distractor is wrong. Do not write any item where more than
one option could be defended as correct.

Note the last sentence — and note that it is not enough. Even with a prompt this constrained, the output is a candidate set.

Step 2 · One generated item

Would you pass this item? (Illustrative example)

Candidate item

A potassium of 6.8 mmol/L is reported on an outpatient sample drawn with a 23-gauge needle, transported by courier in winter, and centrifuged 4 hours after collection. The patient is asymptomatic with a normal ECG. What is the most likely explanation?

A. In-vitro hemolysis from the small-gauge draw
B. Delayed centrifugation with prolonged cell–serum contact
C. Cold-exposure potassium leak during transport
D. True hyperkalemia from unrecognized renal failure

Keyed answer: B. Rationale: potassium leaks from cells when serum is left on the clot…

Step 3 · The gate

The educator verifies exactly one option is defensible

Fails review

Options A, B, and C are all defensible contributors in this stem: a 23-gauge draw can hemolyze, delayed centrifugation raises potassium, and cold transport accelerates the leak. The stem stacks three real pre-analytical causes and then demands a single "most likely" without giving the discriminating detail. A learner graded on this item is being graded on a coin flip.

Disposition

Item returned for revision: keep one pre-analytical insult per stem, or add the discriminating finding (e.g., hemolysis index / visible pink serum) that makes exactly one answer correct. The model is an item drafter operating under an expert gate — never the finalizer of an item a learner will be graded or misled by.

The rest of the pillar

  • Curriculum & session design. A model outlines a four-week rotation in seconds; the educator edits for feasibility, local resources, and accuracy. The reframing — from author to editor — is where the time is actually saved. Milestone/EPA crosswalks come back as hypotheses, with confidence labeled, for faculty to correct.
  • Presenter support. Story arcs, slide sequences, sharper titles — yes. AI-fabricated histology or scientific diagrams — never. The presenter owns every claim on every slide.
  • Role stratification. Faculty draft under expert review; trainees get explicit academic-integrity expectations; presenters use AI for structure and language, not unverified content.

Three guardrails across all of it: content validity and blueprint alignment, active bias screening, and human accountability for every AI-assisted material that reaches learners.
