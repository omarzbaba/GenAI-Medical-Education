Anatomy of an effective prompt

The quality of what comes out of an LLM is strongly influenced by how the task is framed — prompt structure measurably affects the quality and validity of generated content. A well-formed prompt has five components. Toggle any of them off below and watch what the model is left to assume.

Build the prompt

You are a hematopathology attending coaching a resident before
sign-out.
I am a PGY-2 on my second month of heme, and tomorrow we sign out
several marrow cases with possible myelodysplastic syndrome. I am
comfortable with normal marrow morphology but shaky on dysplasia
thresholds.
Explain how the current WHO classification distinguishes MDS from
reactive dysplasia, then walk me through the three findings that
most change the diagnosis.
Keep it under 400 words: one big-picture paragraph, then a numbered
list of the three findings with one sentence each on why it matters.
Name the classification edition you are using, and flag any
threshold or percentage you are not certain about as [VERIFY]
rather than stating it as fact.

Iteration, not incantation

Prompts improve through iteration — draft, observe, refine — rather than in a single pass. The first draft is rarely the one to keep, and refinement continues until the output holds up and has been verified. Two habits from the manuscript's governance layer belong in the prompt itself:

  • Name the version. Many tasks ask a model to apply an external rubric — a classification, a protocol, a milestone framework — and those rubrics are revised on their own schedule. A prompt that encodes a superseded standard produces fluent output measured against the wrong yardstick, and nothing in the response signals it.
  • Ask for uncertainty. A verification cue does not make the model honest; it makes the places to check visible. Verification itself still falls to you.

Prompting deserves explicit teaching as a practical AI-literacy skill — it is a competency the learner carries into every pillar of the framework. For a teachable one-hour version, see the guide How to write a good prompt.
