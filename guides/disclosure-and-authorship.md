If you use AI to help draft a manuscript, a poster, a teaching slide, a letter of recommendation, an abstract, or a grant — when and how do you disclose it? This guide is the working answer for pathology educators, with current journal policy, conference norms, and practical worked examples.

The short version: **AI is a tool, not an author. Use it freely. Disclose its use when it materially shaped the work. Never cite it as a source.** Below is the long version, with the cases that aren't obvious.

---

## Part 1 — The settled questions

### AI cannot be a named author

This is now established across the major medical journals via the ICMJE (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors) Recommendations and adopted by JAMA, NEJM, Lancet, BMJ, and almost every reputable pathology journal:

- An author must be a human capable of taking responsibility for the work
- An author must be capable of approving the final version
- An author must be capable of being held accountable for the work

A language model meets none of these criteria. It cannot take responsibility, cannot approve, cannot be held accountable. Therefore: AI is not an author.

This is true regardless of how substantial the AI's contribution was. Even if a chatbot wrote 80% of the prose, the named authors are the humans who used the tool, reviewed the output, made the final calls, and stand behind the work.

### AI cannot be cited as a primary source

You do not cite "Claude (Anthropic, 2026)" or "ChatGPT" as a reference for a factual claim in scholarly work. AI outputs are not primary sources; they are derived from training data without traceable provenance. To cite the claim, find and cite the actual paper, guideline, or textbook the model was paraphrasing.

If you cannot trace a claim back to a primary source, the claim should not appear in your scholarly work — full stop, whether the AI said it or not.

### AI use must be disclosed when it materially shaped the work

All major journals now require some form of disclosure when AI tools were used. The threshold is "*materially shaped*": if removing the AI's contribution would meaningfully change the manuscript, disclose it. If the AI was used only for spelling, grammar, or trivial copy-editing, most policies do not require disclosure (though some do — read your target journal's policy).

## Part 2 — What journals currently require

Policies vary; check the specific journal before submission. Common patterns as of 2026:

**JAMA and JAMA Network journals.** Disclosure required in Methods (for research articles) or Acknowledgments (for other formats). Must specify which AI tool, which version, the date of use, and what it was used for. AI cannot be a co-author.

**NEJM.** Disclosure required. Specifies that authors are responsible for the accuracy of all content, AI-generated or not. Discourages use of AI for writing substantial portions of original research manuscripts but does not categorically prohibit.

**Lancet.** Disclosure required in the Acknowledgments section, specifying tool and how it was used. AI cannot be a listed author.

**BMJ.** Disclosure required. BMJ has been more open than some about AI-assisted writing as long as authors take responsibility.

**Pathology-specific journals (AJSP, Modern Pathology, Histopathology, Human Pathology, etc.).** Generally aligned with ICMJE: disclosure required, AI not an author. Check individual journal policy.

**Pre-prints (medRxiv, bioRxiv).** Follow journal policy of intended submission target. Disclose proactively even at pre-print stage.

### Why this matters: enforcement

Many journals now require an explicit attestation at submission ("Did you use AI tools in preparing this manuscript? If yes, describe."). Lying on this attestation is misconduct. Editorial responses to undisclosed AI use have ranged from required revision and disclosure to retraction.

The cost of disclosing is zero. The cost of not disclosing and being caught is your scholarly reputation. Disclose.

## Part 3 — Worked examples of disclosure language

The right disclosure is specific enough to be useful and brief enough not to dominate the manuscript.

### Example 1 — AI used for drafting prose in Discussion

> *AI disclosure.* The authors used Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic) in February 2026 to assist with drafting the Discussion section. The authors reviewed, edited, and verified all AI-assisted text and take full responsibility for its accuracy and intellectual content.

Goes in the Methods or Acknowledgments section per the journal's policy.

### Example 2 — AI used for editing existing text

> *AI disclosure.* The authors used GPT-4o (OpenAI) for copy-editing and grammar review. No portion of the manuscript was generated by AI; the tool was used only for refinement of human-written text. The authors take responsibility for the final version.

### Example 3 — AI used in data analysis or figure generation

> *AI disclosure.* Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.4 with the assistance of Claude Opus 4.7 (Anthropic) for code review. The analytical code is available on request. The authors verified all analytical results independently.

### Example 4 — AI not used

> *AI disclosure.* No generative AI tools were used in the preparation of this manuscript.

Some journals require this affirmative statement even when AI was not used. Read the policy.

### Example 5 — AI used for a teaching slide deck or grand rounds

For non-manuscript work, disclosure is often less formal but still expected. A footer line on the slide is sufficient:

> *Slides prepared with assistance from Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic). Content reviewed and verified by the presenter.*

For posters and conference presentations, include the disclosure on the materials section of the poster or in the methods of the abstract.

## Part 4 — The gray-zone cases

### "I used it for spelling and grammar only"

Most journals do not require disclosure for this level of use. But the line between "grammar only" and "rewriting for clarity" is blurry. When in doubt, disclose. The downside of unnecessary disclosure is zero; the downside of under-disclosing is real.

### "I used it to brainstorm ideas, but I wrote everything"

If the conceptual structure of the work was meaningfully shaped by AI suggestion ("the AI suggested I frame the discussion around three themes, which I adopted"), disclose. If you used the AI for unrelated thinking and the manuscript stands on its own intellectual structure, you can choose not to disclose.

### "I used it to translate from another language"

Disclose. AI translation can subtly change meaning. The reader has a right to know.

### "A co-author used it and I didn't"

The senior author is responsible for the manuscript as a whole. If any co-author used AI in their contribution, the senior author must know and the disclosure must reflect it. Establish a "did anyone use AI?" check during manuscript review with your team.

### "I used the AI to generate code/scripts for analysis"

Disclose under Methods. AI-generated code that runs your statistical analysis is a methodologically relevant use. Treat it like you'd treat any other analytical tool — name it, version it, and ideally share the code.

### "I used it to write the letter of recommendation for a resident"

Sticky. There is no formal journal policy here, but the social norm is clear: a letter of recommendation is supposed to reflect the recommender's personal knowledge of the candidate, expressed in the recommender's voice. If the AI drafted the letter substantially, the recipient should know.

The right pattern: use AI as a *prompt* for your own writing (e.g., "Help me brainstorm what to emphasize about this candidate based on my notes"), then write the letter yourself in your own voice. Disclose to the candidate if you used AI in the process, especially if it substantially shaped the framing.

### "I used it for a manuscript I'm submitting to a journal that doesn't have an AI policy"

Disclose anyway. Lack of policy is not lack of expectation; it's policy lag. Disclose using the standard language above. Future you will be glad.

## Part 5 — Boards, licensing, and educational integrity

Different rules apply when the question is "did you write this yourself" in the context of certification or evaluation.

**Boards and licensing exams.** AI assistance is generally prohibited. ABMS / ABPath positions on this evolve; check current rules. Bring nothing AI-related into the exam.

**Resident scholarship requirements.** Most program directors expect AI use to be disclosed for any "scholarly" requirement (case reports, posters, presentations). Some require AI not be used for the writing portion specifically. Ask your PD.

**ACGME milestones evaluation.** AI-drafted CCC narrative comments are a real practice. Most institutions don't yet have explicit policy; the working norm is "the attending takes responsibility for the final content." Disclose if asked.

**Promotion portfolios.** Your home institution's promotion committee will increasingly want to know how much of your scholarly output is AI-assisted. Be prepared to answer.

**Teaching evaluations and CV statements.** Listing AI-assisted publications on your CV is fine; you're listing publications, not claiming AI didn't help. But don't claim sole authorship of work that was substantially AI-drafted.

## Part 6 — Pathology-specific norms

A few patterns specific to pathology that are still evolving:

**Case reports.** AI-assisted drafting is common and generally acceptable with disclosure. Be especially careful that the case description hasn't been "smoothed" in ways that drift from what actually happened with the patient.

**Image interpretation in manuscripts.** Do not use AI to interpret photomicrographs that will appear in your manuscript without disclosing it. The reader assumes a human pathologist made the interpretive call. If AI was used, say so.

**Diagnostic guidelines and consensus statements.** Group authorship work has more complicated AI disclosure norms. Establish whether AI was used at the working-group level; document it; disclose it.

**Teaching files.** Public-facing teaching files (institutional websites, board prep materials, lecture archives) should disclose AI use where material. A footnote line is sufficient.

**Sign-out documentation.** AI use for drafting routine reports is increasingly common. Institutional policy varies wildly. Follow your institution's policy precisely; do not freelance.

## Part 7 — When ghost-writing crosses into misconduct

There's a line between "AI-assisted" and "AI-written." Most journal policies tolerate the former with disclosure; none tolerate the latter without acknowledgment.

Signs you've crossed the line:

- You can't defend specific claims in the manuscript without consulting the AI again
- You don't understand the argument structure
- You can't identify the primary sources behind cited claims
- A reviewer asks you to clarify a passage and you have to re-prompt to figure out what was meant

If any of these are true, you are not the author of that work in any meaningful sense. Either learn it well enough to defend it, or remove it from the manuscript.

A useful test: can you give a 5-minute extempore talk on the manuscript's main points without notes? If not, the work has gotten out ahead of your understanding. AI-assisted writing should accelerate your scholarship, not replace your thinking.

## Part 8 — Practical workflow

For any AI-assisted manuscript, before submission:

1. **Inventory your AI use.** What tools? What versions? What dates? What was used for? Write it down in your project notes during the process — don't reconstruct from memory.
2. **Verify every numerical claim** in the manuscript against a primary source. AI-assisted text is especially prone to threshold drift and citation errors.
3. **Verify every citation.** Open the actual paper or guideline. Confirm the claim is there.
4. **Read the whole manuscript aloud.** AI-assisted prose sometimes reads beautifully on the page but feels stilted or impersonal aloud. Edit the rough spots.
5. **Defend the manuscript verbally** to a co-author or trusted colleague. If you can't talk through any section without consulting the AI, that section needs more work from you.
6. **Write the disclosure statement** before submission, in the form your target journal expects. Don't leave it for the cover letter at 11 PM.
7. **Check your target journal's policy** the week of submission. Policies update frequently.

---

## Part 9 — One closing thought

AI in academic writing is here. Pretending it isn't, or claiming you don't use it when you do, is the worst posture. So is the opposite extreme — letting AI write things you can't defend.

The right posture is the one that's always been right for any tool in scholarly work: use it openly, take responsibility for the output, disclose when relevant, and never claim work you didn't do.

If you can hold that line, the question of "AI authorship" becomes uninteresting. You are the author; the AI helped you; that's noted; everyone moves on.
