One governance layer, not four sets of caveatsImplementation & Guardrails

The same failure modes recur across learning, teaching, scholarship, and operations — so the guardrails are treated as a single governance layer.

Whatever the tool produces, a person remains accountable for it.

The five recurring hazards

Accuracy

Fluent text whether or not the claim is correct: fabricated references, superseded guideline language, confident error on image-anchored and integrative content. Every factual assertion is provisional until checked against a primary source.

Confidentiality

Public consumer interfaces may retain and reuse submitted text. Clinical work is saturated with identifiable data; scholarship adds unpublished IP and material under review. The operative question at every prompt: what may this specific text safely be exposed to?

Bias & equity

Generated cases can encode stereotype — default ages, sexes, races, resource assumptions — so screen actively. And access itself is unequal: paid subscriptions, connectivity, and English proficiency can widen training inequities. Prefer exercises runnable on free models.

Overreliance

The learner-specific hazard. Verification presupposes the knowledge to judge what is being verified — used too early, an answer-supplying tool can prevent the very competence it appears to support.

Accountability

AI cannot be an author and cannot take responsibility. Disclosure of AI assistance is an integrity obligation; the person prompting the model is the person who verifies — and answers for — its output.

Two kinds of drift to design for. Models are non-deterministic and version-dependent — the same prompt behaves differently across tools and dates. And the rubrics prompts invoke (classifications, protocols, review criteria) are revised on their own schedule. Name the version in the prompt; re-check when the governing body revises. Rubric currency is a maintenance obligation, not a one-time setup step.

Can this go into a public consumer tool?

Pick the material you're about to paste. The same rule applies across all four pillars (manuscript Table 6).

Generally acceptable
Explaining a staining pattern, a classification change, a mechanism — general knowledge with no patient or proprietary content. Still verify the output before you rely on it.
Generally acceptable
Already-public information. Remember the retrieval rule, though: models fabricate citations, so the literature itself comes from PubMed/Scopus, not from the model.
Generally acceptable
Fully hypothetical or de-identified teaching cases you constructed yourself are the framework's standard working material. Label them as hypothetical in the prompt, and screen generated details for stereotyped attributes.
Generally acceptable
Your own study notes and practice questions, provided they contain no patient data. Text you authored and have already made public is fine as well.
Keep it out — no exceptions
Protected health information and patient identifiers — names, record or accession numbers, dates, locations. Public consumer tools are not HIPAA-appropriate, and no privacy toggle substitutes for a Business Associate Agreement. Use a covered institutional deployment, or fully de-identify first.
Keep it out
Real case material that is not fully de-identified — including whole-slide images or gross photographs tied to a patient. De-identify completely, or use a sanctioned deployment.
Keep it out
Unpublished data, hypotheses, specific aims, and other intellectual property. This is why pre-submission critique belongs in an institutional or sanctioned tool whose terms exclude training — not a public consumer account.
Keep it out — editorial policy
A manuscript or grant you are peer-reviewing is confidential by editorial policy. Reviewers must not upload assigned manuscripts to public AI tools. This is a governance requirement, not a preference.
Follow the agreement first
Anything under an IRB protocol, sponsor agreement, data-use agreement, or institutional confidentiality obligation follows those terms. When the task touches protected data and no compliant deployment exists, the correct response is not a workaround but abstention: de-identify, use a sanctioned tool, or complete the task without AI.
When a public tool is the only option — the precautions (Table 7)
  • Opt out of model training wherever the setting exists.
  • Use temporary/incognito mode for one-off sensitive prompts.
  • Secure the account: unique password, two-factor authentication — a compromised login is a likelier exposure route than the vendor.
  • Paste the minimum necessary; delete conversations you no longer need.
  • Don't rate sensitive chats with thumbs-up/down feedback.
  • Never enter PHI, patient identifiers, or images tied to a real case.
  • Never upload a manuscript or grant you are reviewing.
  • Treat even opted-out or "temporary" chats as semi-private.
  • Re-check settings and vendor terms periodically — defaults change.
  • When in doubt, stop: de-identify, move to a sanctioned deployment, or do the task without AI.

The single highest-leverage habit is the simplest: a model can only expose what it was given, so the most reliable protection is to withhold sensitive material in the first place. The technical settings are backstops behind that discipline, not substitutes for it.

Match the tool tier to the task

Four tiers, not interchangeable — and the differences carry directly into teaching (manuscript Table 3). Tier selection is part of the pedagogical question, not a default left on the most familiar app.

General-purpose LLMs (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini)
The most accessible and most-studied tier: text-based concept explanation, board-style Q&A, quizzing, study-plan drafting. Limitation: uneven on image-anchored and integrative content; prone to hallucinated facts and fabricated references; outputs vary across products, versions, and runs.
Medical-domain LLMs (e.g., Med-PaLM-class builds)
Instruction-tuned biomedical models sit above generic ones on clinical language tasks — terminology-dense explanation and knowledge retrieval. Limitation: still text-only, so the visual-grounding gap persists; access is less turnkey than consumer chatbots.
Domain foundation models (e.g., Virchow-class, for pathology)
Image–text models trained on large domain image collections bring genuine visual grounding: morphology retrieval, slide-level pattern exposure — the work text-only models handle poorly. Limitation: not conversational tutors on their own; educational validation is limited; foundation-model-specific concerns (bias, accountability) apply.
Domain copilots (e.g., PathChat-class vision–language assistants)
Case-style image-plus-text interaction approximating a subspecialty tutor; in blinded evaluation, a pathology copilot was preferred over a general vision–language model on open-ended responses. Limitation: early-stage, narrowly available; outputs still require expert verification; educational outcomes not yet rigorously evaluated.

Protecting skill formation

Three risks when trainees rely on AI — and the design response is the same for all three: foundational competence before AI augmentation, with the tool used to test reasoning rather than supply it.

Deskilling

Erosion of a competence an experienced clinician already holds. Not hypothetical: unaided adenoma detection declined measurably among endoscopists after routine exposure to AI-assisted colonoscopy (28.4% → 22.4%).

Mis-skilling

Uncritical acceptance of AI errors — a trainee absorbs an incorrect immunoprofile or a superseded criterion and carries it forward as something learned.

Never-skilling

The one training must guard against most: a foundational competence never forms at all, because AI performed the task during the formative window. The trainee is fluent in prompting but not in the reasoning beneath it — false proficiency that surfaces in AI-free, high-stakes assessment.

Foundation-first

Foundational knowledge before AI augmentation.

For the first months, the resident works cases with no AI assistance at all, building the skill through teaching and cases; an AI tutor is switched on only once that foundation is established. (Assistance dilemma; cognitive load theory.)

Attempt-first prompting

Form your own answer, then ask for critique.

Before querying the model about a case, write down your own differential and reasoning, then ask the model to critique it — rather than asking for the answer cold. (Productive failure.)

Staged autonomy

More AI latitude as competence grows.

A subspecialty fellow may pressure-test differentials on active cases; a first-year is limited to self-checks on already-taught material. Guidance is calibrated to expertise and faded as it grows. (Expertise-reversal effect.)

Adopting the framework incrementally

  1. Start low-risk: self-education first.
  2. Keep a brief correction log of model errors your group catches.
  3. Write a one-page local AI-use policy — including academic-integrity expectations for learners. Professional scaffolding exists (e.g., the AAMC principles for responsible AI use), but departments must localize it.
  4. Maintain a shared, version-controlled prompt library. (This site is one; adapt it.)
  5. Want evidence before wider adoption? Run the tool on work whose outcome is already known — a proposal with its summary statement in hand, a published paper with its reviewer reports — and compare what the model surfaced against what human reviewers actually wrote. No new infrastructure, no new approvals.
  6. Be transparent with learners: say when material was AI-drafted, what the faculty verification step was, and what remains uncertain. Concealing AI involvement models exactly the opacity the field is trying to avoid.

Together these practices define a competency rather than a checklist: judging when to trust an output, how to verify it, which tool fits the task, and how to disclose its use. The durable skill is appraisal, not familiarity with any particular product.
