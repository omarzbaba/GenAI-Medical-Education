One governance layer, not four sets of caveatsImplementation & Guardrails

The same failure modes recur across learning, teaching, scholarship, and operations — so the guardrails are treated as a single governance layer.

Whatever the tool produces, a person remains accountable for it.

The five recurring hazards Manuscript · §7 & Supplementary Note 1

Accuracy

Fluent text whether or not the claim is correct: fabricated references, superseded guideline language, confident error on image-anchored and integrative content. Every factual assertion is provisional until checked against a primary source.

Confidentiality

Public consumer interfaces may retain and reuse submitted text. Clinical work is saturated with identifiable data; scholarship adds unpublished IP and material under review. The operative question at every prompt: what may this specific text safely be exposed to?

Bias & equity

Generated cases can encode stereotype — default ages, sexes, races, resource assumptions — so screen actively. And access itself is unequal: paid subscriptions, connectivity, and English proficiency can widen training inequities. Prefer exercises runnable on free models.

Overreliance

The learner-specific hazard. Verification presupposes the knowledge to judge what is being verified — used too early, an answer-supplying tool can prevent the very competence it appears to support.

Accountability

AI cannot be an author and cannot take responsibility. Disclosure of AI assistance is an integrity obligation; the person prompting the model is the person who verifies — and answers for — its output.

Two kinds of drift to design for. Models are non-deterministic and version-dependent — the same prompt behaves differently across tools and dates. And the rubrics prompts invoke (classifications, protocols, review criteria) are revised on their own schedule. Name the version in the prompt; re-check when the governing body revises. Rubric currency is a maintenance obligation, not a one-time setup step.

Can this go into a public consumer tool?

Pick the material you're about to paste. The same rule applies across all four pillars. Manuscript · Table 6

Generally acceptable
General concepts, mechanisms, and definitions (e.g., explaining a staining pattern or a classification change). Still verify the output before you rely on it.
Generally acceptable
Published literature and other already-public information. Remember the retrieval rule, though: models fabricate citations, so the literature itself comes from PubMed/Scopus, not from the model.
Generally acceptable
Fully hypothetical or de-identified teaching cases you constructed yourself. Label them as hypothetical in the prompt, and screen generated details for stereotyped attributes.
Generally acceptable
Your own study notes and practice questions that contain no patient data. Text you authored and have already made public is likewise acceptable.
Keep it out — no exceptions
Protected health information or any patient identifiers — names, medical-record or accession numbers, dates, or locations. Public consumer tools are not HIPAA-appropriate, and no privacy toggle substitutes for a Business Associate Agreement. Use a covered institutional deployment, or fully de-identify first.
Keep it out
Real case material that is not fully de-identified, including whole-slide images or gross photographs tied to a patient. De-identify completely, or use a sanctioned deployment.
Keep it out
Unpublished data, hypotheses, specific aims, or other intellectual property. This is why pre-submission critique belongs in an institutional or sanctioned tool whose terms exclude training — not a public consumer account.
Keep it out — editorial policy
A manuscript or grant you are peer-reviewing, which is confidential by editorial policy. Reviewers must not upload assigned manuscripts to public AI tools. This is a governance requirement, not a preference.
Follow the agreement first
Anything under an IRB protocol, sponsor agreement, data-use agreement, or institutional confidentiality obligation. When the task touches protected data and no compliant deployment exists, the correct response is not a workaround but abstention: de-identify, use a sanctioned tool, or complete the task without AI.
When a public tool is the only option — the precautions Manuscript · Table 7
  • Opt out of model training in the tool's settings wherever the option exists.
  • Use temporary or incognito mode for any one-off sensitive prompt.
  • Secure the account with a unique password and two-factor authentication — a compromised login is a likelier exposure route than the vendor itself.
  • Paste the minimum necessary, and delete conversations you no longer need.
  • Do not rate sensitive chats with thumbs-up/down feedback.
  • Never enter protected health information, patient identifiers, or images tied to a real case.
  • Never upload a manuscript or grant you are reviewing — peer-review material is confidential.
  • Treat even opted-out or “temporary” chats as semi-private.
  • Re-check settings and vendor terms periodically — defaults, menus, and policies change frequently.
  • When in doubt, stop: de-identify, move to a sanctioned deployment, or complete the task without AI.

The single highest-leverage habit is the simplest: a model can only expose what it was given, so the most reliable protection is to withhold sensitive material in the first place. The technical settings are backstops behind that discipline, not substitutes for it.

Match the tool tier to the task

Four tiers, not interchangeable — and the differences carry directly into teaching. Tier selection is part of the pedagogical question, not a default left on the most familiar app. Manuscript · Table 3

General-purpose LLMs (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini)
Text-based concept explanation, board-style question answering, quizzing, and study-plan drafting; the most accessible and most extensively evaluated tier for self-directed learners. Limitation: uneven on image-anchored and integrative content, and prone to hallucinated facts and fabricated references; outputs vary across products, versions, and runs.
Medical-domain LLMs (instruction-tuned biomedical builds, e.g., Med-PaLM)
Biomedical text tasks where domain fine-tuning improves accuracy over generic models, such as terminology-dense explanation and knowledge retrieval. Limitation: still text-only, so the visual-grounding gap persists; access and local deployment are less turnkey than consumer chatbots.
Pathology foundation models (histopathology image–text models, e.g., Virchow)
Image-anchored teaching that text-only models handle poorly, including morphology retrieval and slide-level pattern exposure. Limitation: not conversational tutors on their own; educational validation is limited, and they raise foundation-model–specific ethical concerns such as bias and accountability.
Pathology copilots (PathChat and comparable vision–language case assistants)
Case-style, image-plus-text interaction that approximates a subspecialty tutor for visual reasoning; in blinded evaluation of open-ended responses, a pathology copilot was preferred over a general vision-language model. Limitation: early-stage and narrowly available; outputs still require expert verification, and educational outcomes have not been rigorously evaluated.

Protecting skill formation

Three risks when trainees rely on AI — and the design response is the same for all three: foundational competence before AI augmentation, with the tool used to test reasoning rather than supply it. Manuscript · Supplementary Tables S1–S2

Deskilling

Erosion of a competence an experienced clinician already holds, through over-reliance or disuse. A senior pathologist whose unaided morphologic pattern recognition dulls after routinely accepting model-generated interpretations. Not hypothetical: unaided adenoma detection declined measurably among endoscopists after routine AI-assisted colonoscopy (28.4% → 22.4%).

Mis-skilling

Uncritical acceptance of AI errors, so the trainee internalises flawed knowledge as fact. A trainee who absorbs a chatbot's incorrect immunoprofile or a superseded criterion and carries it forward as something learned.

Never-skilling

A foundational competence that never forms at all, because AI performed the task during the formative window. A trainee who elicits a polished differential from a chatbot but never learns to construct one from the slide. The one training must guard against most.

Foundation-first

Foundational knowledge before AI augmentation.

For the first months of training, the resident works up anemias with no AI assistance at all, building the skill through teaching and cases; an AI tutor is switched on only once that foundation is established. (Assistance dilemma; cognitive load theory.)

Attempt-first prompting

The learner forms their own answer, then compares it against the model's critique.

Before querying the model about a case, the resident writes down their own differential and reasoning, then asks the model to critique it — rather than asking for the diagnosis cold. (Productive failure.)

Staged autonomy

More AI latitude as competence grows.

A subspecialty fellow may use the tool to pressure-test a differential on active cases, while a first-year is limited to a self-check on already-taught material. (Expertise-reversal effect.)

Adopting the framework incrementally Manuscript · §7

  1. Start low-risk: self-education first.
  2. Keep a brief correction log of model errors your group catches.
  3. Write a one-page local AI-use policy — including academic-integrity expectations for learners. Professional scaffolding exists (e.g., the AAMC principles for responsible AI use), but departments must localize it.
  4. Maintain a shared, version-controlled prompt library. (This site is one; adapt it.)
  5. Want evidence before wider adoption? Run the tool on work whose outcome is already known — a proposal with its summary statement in hand, a published paper with its reviewer reports — and compare what the model surfaced against what human reviewers actually wrote. No new infrastructure, no new approvals.
  6. Be transparent with learners: say when material was AI-drafted, what the faculty verification step was, and what remains uncertain. Concealing AI involvement models exactly the opacity the field is trying to avoid.

Together these practices define a competency rather than a checklist: judging when to trust an output, how to verify it, which tool fits the task, and how to disclose its use. The durable skill is appraisal, not familiarity with any particular product.
